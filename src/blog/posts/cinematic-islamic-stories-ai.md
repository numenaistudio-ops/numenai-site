  3. NICHE DNA (why these videos work)
  The 6-beat emotional formula
  1. Shock hook (first 2–4s): start mid-conflict at peak emotion: a shout, a slammed door, a dropped plate, a cruel word. No setup.
  2. The wound: the hero is hurt by, or hurts, someone they love who doesn't understand, can't remember or seems ungrateful. The gentle character stays gentle.
  3. Collapse + hard truth: a silent breakdown (sliding down a wall, crying alone in a car, sitting on a prayer mat). Then the truth arrives through a doctor, a letter, a phone call or a discovered object.
  4. Search / turning point: something goes wrong (someone goes missing, falls ill or leaves). The hero rushes through the night, and a kind stranger or neighbour points the way.
  5. Golden flashback: a warm golden memory of the sacrifice the other person made years ago, with one key object and one promise line.
  6. Mirror payoff: the roles reverse. The hero repeats the promise line (adapted) and the key object passes back. A quiet moment of recognition or forgiveness, then a final wide shot under warm light.
  The emotional engine: a past sacrifice returned. The same line and the same object appearing in the past and the present is what makes viewers cry.
  Theme bank (always build a fresh, original angle)
  Aging parents, a father's hidden sacrifice, an orphan and a kind stranger, a sibling who gave up a dream, neighbours' rights, a child who ignored a parent's calls, forgiveness, honesty when no one is watching, sadaqah returning, gratitude, patience, caring for the elderly, Ramadan and Eid, a teacher's quiet kindness, a lost child and a stranger.
  Pacing
  * Short lines of 3–10 words. Long silences on the saddest beats.
  * 4s clips carry hooks, reactions, inserts, running shots and silent emotional beats.
  * 6s clips carry dialogue exchanges, the truth reveal, the flashback promise and the mirror payoff.
  * Dialogue limits: about 8 words max in a 4s clip (one speaker). About 14 words max in a 6s clip (up to two short lines).
  Visual devices
  Night vs golden-flashback colour contrast; tears in close-up; a soft white flash in and out of flashbacks and time jumps; one warm street lamp; wet reflective ground; hands holding hands.
  Audio
  A soft vocals-only nasheed, wordless humming or gentle ambient pads under the story. The music drops to silence just before the biggest line, then swells. Flashbacks get a warmer, dreamier music cue.

  5. CHARACTER RULES
  * Every project gets a new cast with new names, faces, ages, builds, outfits and voices. Vary ethnicity (Pakistani, Indian, Bangladeshi, Arab, Turkish, Somali, Malay, Indonesian, convert families and so on), age mix and family structure from story to story.
  * Each character gets one dense locked descriptor sentence built from this template: "[Name]: a stylized Pixar-style 3D [ethnicity] [man/woman/boy/girl] [age], [build], [skin tone] skin, [hair style and colour], [facial hair / hijab style and colour / kufi], [eye detail], [one unique mark e.g. mole, scar, grey streak, freckles], wearing [every clothing item with colour and fit], [accessories], [footwear]"
  * Also give each character: Voice (age, gender, accent, pitch, pace, texture, emotion), Personality (one line), Role in the story (one line), a Reference file name (CHAR_[Name]).
  * Voices are locked like descriptors (VOICE LOCK). Each speaking character gets one locked voice sentence, pasted word for word into every Flow clip they speak in, across every episode. A younger version gets its own voice lock.
  * VOICE LOCK template (one dense sentence, no vague words like "nice" or "normal"): "[Name]'s voice: [gender], [exact age], [accent, e.g. British Pakistani / soft Arab-accented English], [pitch: low / medium-low / medium / high], [pace: slow / measured / quick], [timbre: warm, gravelly, breathy, husky, clear, nasal], [volume habit: soft-spoken / firm], [one signature trait, e.g. slight rasp on long words, gentle smile in the voice, slight lisp], speaking clear English"
  * Make voices easy to tell apart: give every character a clearly different combination of age, pitch and timbre, so Flow is less likely to mix them up.
  * Emotion goes in the delivery field, never in the voice lock. The voice stays the same while the emotion changes per line (whispering, crying, shouting).
  * Younger or older versions (flashbacks or time jumps) keep the same unique mark and the same signature colour so viewers connect them instantly. Each version is a separate character entry with its own reference image.
  * Episodic series: the cast and descriptors stay locked across every episode. New characters may join, each with a new locked descriptor and reference image.

  * Faith words where they fit naturally: Bismillah, Alhamdulillah, InshaAllah, SubhanAllah, Ya Allah, Assalamu alaikum, JazakAllah khair.
  * Line patterns (write new wording every time):
     * the pain line is a question
     * the truth line is short and calm
     * the innocent line reveals love without knowing it
     * the promise line is said in the flashback and echoed at the end
     * the climax is often one name, whispered

Today, you can create a complete narrative video featuring expressive characters, heartfelt dialogue, and cinematic lighting from your own computer using accessible AI tools.

Islamic moral stories have become widely watched across social platforms because viewers connect deeply with themes of family, patience, kindness, and faith. In this guide, you will follow a step-by-step workflow to produce your own 3D cinematic Islamic story using a single structured master prompt.

## What you'll make

* A complete cinematic 3D Islamic moral story with an emotional narrative arc.
* Consistent original characters designed across different ages and viewpoints.
* Thirteen cinematic scene images that serve as direct animation starting points.
* Lip-synced video clips generated in batches to stay within credit allowances.

## What you need

* **Claude AI**: Used to brainstorm story concepts, build character bibles, format scripts, and write animation instructions.

* **Google Flow**: Used in Agent Mode to animate the scene images into video clips with lip-synced dialogue.

## Step-by-step

### Step 1: Plan Your Story and Script in Claude AI

Begin by copying the master prompt displayed above this article. Open Claude AI, paste the master prompt into a fresh chat, and start by choosing whether you want to produce a standalone video or an episodic series.

Claude acts as your narrative planner. It will present five original story ideas based around core values like family sacrifice, honesty, or caring for parents. For this example workflow, we follow the story "The Call He Didn't Answer," featuring Adam and his father. Once you pick an idea, choose your aspect ratio, such as vertical 9:16 for short-form video or 16:9 for widescreen, and set your target duration.

Claude then produces a full story bible, detailed character descriptions, locked voice profiles, and a complete scene-by-scene script. The script also includes a credit plan so you know exactly how many four-second and six-second clips you need without exceeding generation limits.

### Step 2: Generate Consistent Characters in Google Gemini

Move to Google Gemini to bring your cast to life. Claude will provide ready-to-use JSON character reference prompts containing locked descriptions of each character's clothing, facial features, age, and unique marks.

Paste each prompt into Gemini to produce a character reference sheet showing your character from multiple angles on a neutral background. If your story requires a younger or older version of the same person for a flashback, attach the primary reference image and keep the same distinctive mark and signature outfit color. Download every image and save them clearly, such as CHAR_Adam, so you can easily reference them later.

### Step 3: Create First-Frame Scene Images

Next, you will generate the opening frame for all thirteen story scenes. Claude supplies a dedicated image prompt for every scene in sequence.

In Gemini, attach your saved character reference sheets before submitting the prompt. This ensures that the generated environment incorporates the exact facial structure, skin tone, and clothing established in your character bible. Each prompt describes a frozen emotional moment, specific camera framing, and lighting setups like warm amber interior lamps or deep blue night exteriors. Review each generated image carefully, download it, and rename it to match its scene number.

### Step 4: Animate Clips in Google Flow Agent Mode

Take your numbered scene images into Google Flow. To work within the standard free limit of 50 credits per batch, Claude organizes your thirteen scenes into manageable batches combining four-second clips (seven credits each) and six-second clips (ten credits each).

Upload the start frames for your current batch into Google Flow and switch to Agent Mode. Paste the full batch text prompt generated by Claude directly into the agent. The agent handles image-to-video generation, maintains locked character voice traits, and syncs lip movement directly to the script dialogue. Once the batch finishes, run the rename prompt provided in your plan so your generated video files stay in exact chronological order.

### Step 5: Check Voice Consistency and Fix Drifts

Listen carefully to each generated video clip to evaluate the audio quality and speech delivery. In most cases, the native voices created by Google Flow will match the locked voice profile assigned in your planning step.

If a character's voice changes pitch or drifts noticeably between scenes, you can easily correct it. Separate the raw audio track in CapCut and upload it to ElevenLabs Voice Changer. Select a matching voice profile to re-voice the line while preserving the original timing, then place the corrected dialogue track directly beneath the video clip so the lip sync remains aligned.

### Step 6: Create Background Music in Suno AI

Cinematic storytelling relies on subtle audio to support emotional moments. Open Suno AI to generate background cues tailored to your scenes. For Islamic stories, set the style parameters to vocals-only nasheeds, gentle wordless humming, and soft ambient choral pads without heavy percussion or instruments.

Generate distinct audio cues for different emotional beats: a melancholic cue for the opening conflict, a gentle acoustic build for flashbacks, and an uplifting swell for the resolution. Download your preferred variations to use in your editing timeline.

### Step 7: Edit and Export in CapCut

Import your numbered video clips into CapCut in chronological order. Lay your background music cues on a separate track beneath your video and sound effects.

Add soft white-flash transitions when shifting into or out of flashback scenes. Lower the background music volume to around minus twenty decibels whenever dialogue is spoken, and introduce brief moments of absolute silence right before major emotional lines to let the words land. Finally, generate bold, centered captions in the lower third of the screen, review the pacing, and export your video in crisp high definition.

## Tips for better results

* **Keep visual descriptions locked**: Always reuse the exact same descriptive sentence for each character across every single prompt to prevent clothing or facial features from shifting.
* **Respect credit thresholds**: Group your animation requests into batches of fifty credits or fewer so you never interrupt a scene midway through a generation run.
* **Use silence for impact**: Drop all background music to complete silence one second before an emotional reveal, allowing the dialogue alone to carry the scene.
* **Structure your file names**: Stick strictly to sequential naming like SC01, SC02, and SC03 so all assets align automatically when imported into your editor.

## FAQ

### Can I make these videos without any paid subscriptions?
Yes, every step in this workflow can be completed using the free tiers available across Claude, Google Gemini, Google Flow, and CapCut.

### How do I prevent character faces from changing between scenes?
Attach your original character reference sheets in Google Gemini before submitting each scene prompt, and ensure the locked character description is included word for word.

### What should I do if a four-second clip feels too rushed for dialogue?
You can adjust your script plan to render that specific scene as a six-second clip in your next batch, giving the line more natural breathing room.
