In this guide, you will learn how to turn an educational idea into a complete paper-cut style documentary video using a single master prompt. This workflow walks you through the entire process step by step, using the example of an educational documentary about an ancient shipwreck discovered near Kekova Island in Turkey.

Cardboard and paper-cut explainer videos are widely watched on video platforms because the handcrafted aesthetic immediately catches the eye. Layered construction paper, visible paper grain, torn white edges, and stop-motion motion cues give factual stories a warm, tactile feel that makes complex history or science topics easy to follow.

## What you'll make

* A complete educational documentary video featuring a multi-layered paper-cut and cardboard visual style.
* A structured script with timed voiceover lines, character descriptions, and scene breakdowns.
* A series of consistent 2D paper illustrations generated from frozen-frame prompts.
* Stop-motion animated video clips using physical paper assembly and parallax effects.
* A fully edited explainer video with synchronized narration and background music.

* **Claude AI**: Used for topic research, scriptwriting, scene breakdowns, and generating structured image and video prompts.
* **Google Gemini**: Used to generate consistent 2D paper-cut and cardboard-style illustrations.
* **Google Flow**: Used with Agent Mode to organize assets and animate your paper-cut scenes.
* **ElevenLabs**: Used to generate clear, professional documentary narration.
* **CapCut**: Used to edit your final video, sync voiceover tracks to animated scenes, adjust speeds, and add background music.

## Step-by-step

### Step 1: Copy and run the master prompt

Start by copying the master prompt displayed right above this guide. Paste it directly into a new chat in Claude AI. The prompt is designed to act as an automated showrunner and art director. It guides you through the entire production pipeline one step at a time, pausing after each stage to wait for your decisions rather than rushing ahead.

### Step 2: Choose your niche and documentary topic

Once the master prompt introduces itself, pick an educational niche such as history, archaeology, geopolitics, or geography. Claude will provide ten story ideas formatted with two-second hooks, story angles, and signature paper visuals. For this demonstration, the topic selected is the ancient shipwreck discovery near Kekova Island, Turkey. You can select one of the suggested ideas or type your own.

### Step 3: Set your video format and clip timing

Next, choose your aspect ratio, such as 16:9 for widescreen YouTube videos or 9:16 for vertical platforms. You will also select your target runtime. The master prompt structures your timeline strictly into 4-second and 6-second clips. For example, a 60-second video uses a combination of 4-second and 6-second scenes (such as eleven clips total) to keep visual pacing snappy and prevent static moments. Confirm your format to lock in the plan.

### Step 4: Generate the script and scene breakdown

Claude will generate a story outline covering the hook, context, story beats, and payoff. It also creates a visual guide with locked color palettes, material notes, and descriptors for recurring characters or settings. You will get a complete scene table showing clip durations, on-screen action, and exact voiceover lines limited to the clip length. Review the historical details, check the facts, and approve the script.

### Step 5: Generate paper-cut illustrations in Google Gemini

The prompt provides an individual JSON code block for each clip's starting frame. These prompts include specific style descriptions, such as layered cardboard, torn white edges, shadow-box depth, and soft tabletop lighting. Take these prompts into Google Gemini to generate your 2D illustrations. Start by generating your key scene or subject first, then use that image as a visual reference for subsequent prompts to keep character designs and textures consistent across all scenes.

### Step 6: Animate the clips with Google Flow Agent Mode

Open Google Flow and activate Agent Mode to configure your workspace. Feed your approved Gemini illustrations into the tool along with the image-to-video animation prompts provided by the master prompt. These prompts instruct the model to move objects like physical paper—using stepped stop-motion cadence, torn-paper reveals, and soft bounces rather than smooth digital morphing. Generate and review your clips, then rename your video files so they match the scene numbers.

### Step 7: Record the voiceover in ElevenLabs

Take the finalized, timed voiceover text from the master prompt into ElevenLabs. The prompt outlines the recommended tone—such as a calm, confident documentary host—along with pauses and emphasis marks. Generate your narration audio and export the audio file ready for the timeline.

### Step 8: Assemble and synchronize in CapCut

Bring your animated video clips and ElevenLabs narration into CapCut. Place your voiceover on the audio track and arrange your video clips in order. Synchronize the scene transitions to match the natural pauses in the voiceover. If a clip runs slightly long or short, adjust the clip speed to align cleanly with the narration. Finish by adding a subtle background music track that sits quietly beneath the voiceover, and export your completed documentary.

## Tips for better results

* **Lock in a primary visual reference first**: Generate your clearest hero shot in Google Gemini before creating other scenes. Feeding this initial image back in as a reference helps maintain color and paper texture consistency.
* **Stick strictly to 4-second and 6-second clips**: Limiting every clip to either 4 or 6 seconds prevents visual fatigue and keeps the pacing aligned with modern editorial explainers.
* **Keep the style descriptors intact**: Always preserve the prompt text describing torn white paper edges, layered cardstock, and soft directional light to avoid unwanted glossy CGI or photorealistic outputs.
* **Focus on paper motion physics**: When animating in Google Flow, favor simple paper actions such as sliding layers, unfolds, and paper rips rather than complex character motions to maintain the handmade look.
